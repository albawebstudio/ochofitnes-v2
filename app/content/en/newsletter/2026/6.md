## Background & Early Influences

> Q: Ed, thank you for taking the time to answer some questions. So glad I was able to get you to help. Can you tell us a little about your background?

Considering your audience — friends, family, clients, and others who look to you for insight or advice — I think it’s worth knowing what influenced me to become interested in learning how the body works, which specifically led me into exercise physiology, training, and teaching.

The heart of it was, and still is, curiosity about how nature works.

Fortunately, I grew up with both my parents at home. My dad was a self-employed meteorologist who taught me a great deal about observing nature and the world in general. My mom was a full-blooded Italian who cooked fresh food every day for our family of six.

We had three vegetable gardens — two public/community gardens and one in our backyard — so an understanding of real food and where it comes from was built naturally at a young age, when it matters most.

When you grow up eating real food — food that literally grows biologically and doesn’t come out of bags, boxes, or plastic containers — I think it conditions both the tongue and the body to desire real food and reject junk food.

Just as important was having a dad who was incredibly creative and truly a force of nature.

Even though he was a self-employed weatherman, he spoke on many radio stations, appeared on television, wrote books on weather and climate history, created the first weather calendar in the United States, consulted for a Coen Brothers movie, and pioneered forensic meteorology.

He taught me to become an authority in my field. He literally said:

> “Make yourself the authority.”

To do so, he believed you must:
- Be curious
- Work hard
- Care deeply
- Be confident and polite
- Study passionately

After first declaring myself a physics major at the University of Minnesota — and later dropping out — I transferred to Madison, Wisconsin, where I earned a Bachelor of Science degree in Exercise Physiology.

I immediately began training people after graduation, working with kids ages 9–12 at the YMCA in Madison.

Keep in mind, the personal training industry as it exists today barely existed back then. It was in its early stages and ready to explode. The same was true for massage therapy.

This was 1991.

Fast forward to today — I’ve never stopped being a personal trainer.

In the mid-90s I began writing articles on fitness, nutrition, and self-care for a local magazine. I also created hybrid yoga/resistance training workshops and offered them at various venues, including “The Open U,” where Pavel Tsatsouline popularized kettlebell training in his early years.

Then the internet exploded, and suddenly everyone needed a website. I painstakingly built a simple site just to establish an online presence.

At the time, I had developed educational materials I wanted to distribute beyond workshops. Eventually Saint Paul College found my website, purchased some of my materials, and later hired me to teach exercise physiology there — which I did for nine years.

## Why Is Nutrition So Confusing?

> Q: With so much nutritional advice available, it seems like you can find a “nutritionist” for every diet — vegan, carnivore, paleo, keto, and more. Why is eating so hard?

Seeing your question about differing nutritional beliefs and approaches, I must say that by the late 90s I was more driven to teach nutrition than simply train people.

Most of my educational materials focus more on nutrition and physiology than strength training because nutrition is much more abstract and largely invisible compared to physical training.

You don’t actually see nutrition happening.

At its core, nutrition is about transforming energy inside the body from the food we eat — food that powers us and has long been associated with healing, longevity, performance, weight loss, and vitality.

People have always viewed food as medicine.

Because of that, food becomes easy to package into:
- Diets
- Recipes
- Supplements
- Systems
- “Magic solutions”

There’s an endless fascination with food and all the ways it can be manipulated and sold.

The desire for food as healing or transformation is nearly insatiable, which makes it easy to market.

Physical training is much more direct. Poor training is easier to identify. But nutrition is deeply personal, emotional, and tied to identity.

People don’t usually say:
> “Just give me a pill instead of food.”

But they often say:
> “Just give me a pill instead of exercise.”

Food is personal.

That personal connection often influences:
- Which diet someone follows
- Which “nutritionist” they trust
- How people build entire identities around nutrition

Now add in the technical side of nutrition — carbohydrates, glycogen, proteins, fats, metabolism — and it becomes clear why eating well is difficult.

One final point:

People often try to understand food without understanding its most important counterpart:
serious physical training.

When people train consistently and intensely (while safely), food becomes easier to understand because they can directly feel and observe what works.

Dieting without exercise is incomplete.

## Supplements for Active People

> Q: There are more and more supplements being pushed on people. What supplements would you recommend for most active people?

Supplements are simply an extension of our endless fascination with food and performance.

People want highly specific results:
- Health
- Energy
- Recovery
- Performance
- Longevity

So supplements are marketed heavily.

From a general health perspective rather than pure performance, I focus primarily on vitamins and minerals.

### Minerals
- Magnesium is one of the hardest minerals to obtain in effective amounts from food alone.
- I personally buy magnesium citrate powder because it’s cost effective.
- Calcium supplementation usually isn’t necessary if you eat real food consistently.
- Potassium supplements are limited to very small doses, making food a far better source.

### Vitamins
- B vitamins are crucial, especially for active and elderly people.
- Vitamin B12 absorption can be impaired in people taking proton pump inhibitors.
- Vitamin D is important for people who lack sufficient sun exposure.
- Vitamin A can be obtained from liver.
- Vitamin E is excellent in wheat germ oil and sunflower seeds.
- Vitamin K is abundant in leafy greens.
- Vitamin C and zinc also remain important.

The fat-soluble vitamins:
- A
- D
- E
- K

…are particularly important.

## Coffee & Health

> Q: Can you give us your professional opinion on coffee? I love the stuff.

Coffee is my best friend in the morning.

Research clearly shows that a couple cups per day can lower diabetes risk — whether caffeinated or decaf.

Coffee also:
- Speeds us up
- Improves alertness
- Enhances reading speed

However, as people age — especially if they:
- Don’t exercise
- Don’t eat well
- Don’t stay hydrated

…caffeine can become problematic relative to heart rhythm issues like atrial fibrillation.

Caffeine increases calcium flux, which triggers muscle contractions. That can worsen muscle twitching and heart rhythm irregularities.

I also suspect low magnesium contributes to rising rates of A-fib in the U.S.

## Tracking Calories & Meal Planning

> Q: I recently started following a calorie-based diet and realized I wasn’t eating enough. I’m now eating close to 3,000 calories. Do you think calorie calculators are helpful?

Absolutely.

Establishing a framework and understanding the big picture is always smart.

Tracking calories gives you a benchmark.

Without a benchmark, how can you intelligently adjust anything up or down?

Even more important than total calories is understanding your macronutrients:
- Carbohydrates
- Protein
- Fat

People should learn:
- Their intake levels
- Their ratios
- How those ratios support training and recovery

## Under-Eating & Training

> Q: Sometimes I think clients are under-eating. What are the dangers of under-eating while lifting weights or doing hard cardio?

Possible consequences include:
- Poor recovery
- Reduced tissue repair
- Lower immunity
- Increased injury risk
- Physical and mental burnout

A useful reality to understand:

### When trying to gain weight:
You try to minimize fat gain.

### When trying to lose weight:
You try to minimize muscle loss.

## Carbs, Fats & Performance

> Q: There’s still a lot of confusion regarding carbs and fats. What fats are bad? Are there good fats? Why do our bodies need carbs?

Over the last 15 years:
- Saturated fats like beef tallow and lard have made a comeback
- Seed oils have fallen out of favor

Good fat sources include:
- Olive oil
- Butter
- Coconut oil
- Nuts
- Seeds
- Lard

Interestingly, lard is mostly made of oleic acid — the same primary fatty acid found in olive oil.

This is why simplistic “good food vs bad food” thinking often lacks depth.

### Why carbs matter

For performance, carbohydrates are essential because muscles rely on glycogen for intense activity.

Glycogen is simply the body’s stored form of carbohydrate.

The harder and more frequently you train:
- The more glycogen you deplete
- The more carbohydrates you require to replenish it

A sedentary person may stay out of ketosis at around 50g of carbs per day.

But active individuals need significantly more.

A useful benchmark for lean performance training:
- Carb-to-protein ratio: roughly 1:1 to 1.5:1
- Protein intake: about 1g per pound of lean mass

Context matters.

A “low carb” diet means very different things depending on:
- Training intensity
- Glycogen depletion
- Recovery demands

## Is Beef Bad for You?

> Q: I love beef. Some clients have been told eating beef is bad. Is it?

Food often gets judged in simplistic black-and-white terms:
- Good
- Bad

But that mindset prevents deeper understanding.

Beef is a major source of:
- Vitamin B12
- Potassium
- Magnesium

Plants, by comparison, contain virtually no B12.

Some people maintain excellent blood markers while eating beef daily.

I don’t believe there’s a universal answer for “how much is too much.” It likely varies significantly from person to person.

---

## Cholesterol & Eggs

> Q: People think eggs raise cholesterol. Do they?

Roughly 80% of the body’s cholesterol is produced internally by the liver.

This is called an endogenous source.

Eggs provide cholesterol from an exogenous source — meaning outside the body.

For most people, eggs do not significantly raise serum cholesterol levels.
